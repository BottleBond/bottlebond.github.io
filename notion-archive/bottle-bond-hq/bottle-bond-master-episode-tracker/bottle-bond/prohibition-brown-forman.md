### The Rise of Brown-Forman

- **Founding of Brown-Forman**: Established in 1870 by George Garvin Brown, born 1846 - Munfordville, KY.
    - **Innovation**: Old Forester was the first bourbon sold exclusively in sealed glass bottles, ensuring quality and consistency, an innovation that helped build the brand’s reputation for reliability.
        - GGB, a young pharmaceutical salesman starts J.T.S. Brown and Bro. with his 1/2 brother
    - Old Forester was blended from diﬀerent distilleries, including Mattingly, Athertonand Mellwood to create a consistent flavor profile. Old Forester is originallypresented at 90 Proof.
    - Old Forester is named after a prominent physician and friend of GGB, Dr. William Forrester who endorsed Brown’s innovative concept of medicinal whisky sold only in sealed glass bottles. Old Forester, originally spelled with 2 “r”s to be associated with the doctor, drops the second “r” when Forrester retired.
    - 1901 George Garvin Brown moved from blender to distiller with the purchase of B.F. Mattingly Distillery.

### Impact of Prohibition on the Whiskey Industry

- The Prohibition movement was largely a religious movement, Temperence Movement. There were medical authorities on both sides. While Prohibitionist doctors said whiskey was poison, other doctors said it was a useful tonic that they often recommended to their patients.
- So strong was the pro-whiskey position among doctors that an exception was written into the Prohibition law. Within certain limitations, doctors were permitted to write prescriptions for whiskey which patients had filled at a pharmacy just like any other medicine. Today, while most doctors are not hostile to alcohol, and many scientists say moderate consumption is healthier than abstinence, officially whiskey has no medicinal value. That wasn't the case a century ago.
- **Prohibition Hits**: On January 16, 1920, Volstead Act, the sale, production, and distribution of alcoholic beverages became illegal under U.S. law, a devastating blow to many distilleries.
    - **Industry Collapse**
    Prohibition's Impact: Enforced on January 16, 1920, Prohibition outlawed the production, sale, and transportation of alcohol, resulting in the closure of around 90% of the 1,500 distilleries in the U.S. Many family-owned businesses were lost, devastating local economies reliant on distilling.
    Job Losses and Economic Strain: The closure of distilleries led to massive unemployment for workers and farmers supplying raw materials, significantly affecting communities that relied on the industry.
    Rise of Bootlegging: With legal access to alcohol eliminated, illegal bootlegging flourished. Organized crime and individuals produced and distributed spirits, often leading to dangerously low-quality products.
    Speakeasies Emergence: Clandestine bars, known as speakeasies, became popular, allowing people to consume alcohol in secret. This underground culture thrived, blending rebellion with social gatherings and contributing to the jazz age.
    Consequences of Illegal Alcohol: The production of unregulated alcohol led to health crises from poisoning and “rotgut” liquor, raising public health concerns.
    Government Enforcement Challenges: The government struggled to enforce Prohibition laws, with widespread corruption undermining efforts. Many Americans openly defied the restrictions, leading to a growing culture of lawlessness.
    Shift in Public Sentiment: As the negative consequences of Prohibition became evident, public sentiment shifted. The economic turmoil and glamorization of bootlegging led to increasing calls for repeal, culminating in the end of Prohibition in 1933 and the resurgence of legal distilleries like Brown-Forman.
    - **Survival Strategies**: Many companies, including distilleries, had to adapt or face closure.

### Brown-Forman’s Strategy During Prohibition

- **Medicinal Whiskey**: Brown-Forman was one of the few distilleries to receive a coveted federal license to sell whiskey for medicinal purposes during Prohibition.
    - **License No. 3**: Brown-Forman was awarded license No. 3, allowing them to continue producing and selling Old Forester as medicinal whiskey. Doctors could prescribe whiskey, and pharmacies could dispense it.
        - A medicinal license entitled you to sell whiskey made before Prohibition went into effect, which you could legally buy from the whiskey's producer. The license did not entitle you to distill until 1929, when the government allowed three million gallons of spirit per year to be produced by the medicinal license holders. A. Ph. Stitzel, by then under the control of Pappy Van Winkle, was the first distillery to take advantage of that opportunity. Since Prohibition was repealed four years later, little if any of that post-1929 distillate was sold until after repeal, but it gave Stitzel and others a nice head start.
            
            The whiskey sold during Prohibition 'for medicinal purposes only' was very standardized. It always came in a one-pint bottle, in a box of either cardboard or metal.
            
            While whiskey bottled before 1920 is very rare, Prohibition medicinal whiskey is surprisingly common. A bottle of it is a nice historical artifact but little else. The whiskey inside is generally awful.
            
    - **Marketing as Medicine**: The company cleverly continued to promote Old Forester as a health product, a strategy that played a key role in securing their license.
- **Adaptation and Growth**: Instead of merely surviving, Brown-Forman found opportunities to expand during Prohibition.
    - **Acquisition of Early Times**: In 1923, Brown-Forman acquired Early Times whiskey, positioning itself for greater market control after Prohibition ended.

### Brown-Forman After Prohibition

- **Repeal of Prohibition (1933)**: With the end of Prohibition, Brown-Forman was one of the few companies well-positioned to bounce back quickly.
    - **Resilient Brand**: Old Forester was one of the only bourbons continuously sold by the same company before, during, and after Prohibition.
    - **Early Times Dominance**: By the 1950s, Early Times (owned by Brown-Forman) became the best-selling whiskey in America.

### Broader Implications for the Industry

- **Prohibition’s Legacy**: How Prohibition reshaped the whiskey industry, forcing many distilleries to close while others, like Brown-Forman, found innovative ways to survive and thrive.
    - **Rise of Medicinal Whiskey**: This legal loophole kept a handful of distilleries alive, setting the stage for the modern resurgence of American whiskey.

### Brown-Forman’s Enduring Legacy

- **Post-Prohibition Success**: Brown-Forman’s ability to stay operational during Prohibition set the company up for future dominance in the whiskey industry.
    - **Jack Daniel's Acquisition**: In 1956, Brown-Forman acquired Jack Daniel's, another iconic brand that would further cement the company’s place as a whiskey giant.
    - **Expansion Beyond Whiskey**: Over the years, Brown-Forman expanded its portfolio, but the foundation laid by its resilience during Prohibition continues to define the company today.

### Closing Thoughts

- **Historical Significance**: Brown-Forman is not just a distillery that survived Prohibition—it thrived by leveraging smart strategies, legal loopholes, and marketing acumen.
- **Legacy Today**: From medicinal whiskey during Prohibition to leading global brands like Jack Daniel’s and Old Forester, Brown-Forman’s journey is a testament to innovation, resilience, and adaptability in the face of legal and market challenges.

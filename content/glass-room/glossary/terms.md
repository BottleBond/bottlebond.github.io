# Essential Whiskey & Bourbon Terms

Whether you are just starting your bourbon journey or deepening your knowledge, these are the terms you will encounter most often. Bookmark this page and come back whenever you need a refresher.

---

## Angel's Share

The portion of whiskey that evaporates from the barrel during aging. In Kentucky's hot summers, this can be 2-4% per year. Over a decade of aging, a barrel may lose a third or more of its contents to the angels. The angel's share is one reason older whiskeys cost more -- there is simply less left in the barrel.

## Barrel Entry Proof

The proof (alcohol concentration) at which new-make spirit is put into the barrel for aging. The legal maximum for bourbon is 125 proof (62.5% ABV). Different entry proofs extract different flavors from the wood. Lower entry proofs generally produce a richer, more complex spirit.

## Bottled in Bond

A designation regulated by the Bottled-in-Bond Act of 1897. To carry this label, a whiskey must be the product of a single distillery, a single distilling season, aged at least four years in a federally bonded warehouse, and bottled at exactly 100 proof. It was America's first consumer protection law for spirits.

## Cask Strength (Barrel Proof)

Whiskey bottled directly from the barrel without adding water to reduce the proof. Cask strength bourbons typically range from 110 to 140 proof. They offer the most intense, unaltered flavor but can be quite powerful. Adding your own water lets you find your preferred proof.

## Charring Levels

The degree to which the inside of a barrel is burned before filling. Ranges from #1 (light, 15 seconds) to #4 ("alligator char," 55+ seconds). Charring caramelizes wood sugars and creates a carbon filter layer. The char level significantly influences the vanilla, caramel, and smoky notes in the final whiskey.

## Finish

The flavors and sensations that linger after you swallow (or spit) a whiskey. A "long finish" means the flavors persist for many seconds. The finish often reveals different notes than the initial taste -- spice, oak tannins, or warming heat. A quality bourbon typically has a complex, evolving finish.

## Mash Bill

The recipe of grains used to make a whiskey, expressed as percentages. A typical bourbon mash bill might be 75% corn, 13% rye, 12% malted barley. The mash bill is one of the primary factors determining a whiskey's flavor profile. Bourbon must be at least 51% corn by law.

## Proof

A measure of alcohol content equal to twice the ABV (alcohol by volume). An 80 proof bourbon is 40% ABV. The term originated from the practice of "proving" alcohol content by soaking gunpowder in the spirit -- if it could still ignite, it was "proof" of sufficient alcohol content.

## Rickhouse (Rackhouse)

A warehouse specifically designed for aging whiskey barrels. Traditional rickhouses are multi-story wooden or stone structures where barrels are stored on wooden racks called "ricks." Temperature varies significantly between floors, which affects how each barrel ages. Some modern rickhouses use climate control, while others rely on natural temperature swings.

## Single Barrel

A whiskey bottled from one individual barrel rather than blended from multiple barrels. Each single barrel release is unique, reflecting the specific characteristics of that barrel's position in the rickhouse, its particular wood grain, and other variables. No two single barrels are exactly alike.

## Small Batch

A marketing term (not legally defined) indicating a bourbon blended from a relatively small number of barrels. What counts as "small" varies by distillery -- it could mean anywhere from a handful of barrels to a few hundred. The intent is to suggest a more curated, hand-selected product.

## Sour Mash

A process where a portion of previously fermented mash (called "setback" or "backset") is added to a new batch of mash. This is similar to using a sourdough starter in bread. The acidity helps control bacterial growth and ensures consistency from batch to batch. Nearly all bourbon uses the sour mash process, though not all advertise it.

## Straight Bourbon

Bourbon that has been aged for at least two years. If aged less than four years, the age must be stated on the label. Straight bourbon also cannot contain any added coloring, flavoring, or other spirits. This is the most common designation you will see on bourbon bottles.

## White Dog (New Make)

The clear, unaged spirit that comes off the still before it goes into barrels. White dog tastes of raw grain, has a sharp bite, and bears little resemblance to the amber bourbon it will become. Some distilleries sell white dog as a product in its own right, offering a glimpse into the starting point of the aging journey.

## Mashbill

An alternate spelling of "mash bill." Both forms are widely used in the industry. See **Mash Bill** above for the full definition.
